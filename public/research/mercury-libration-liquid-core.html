Mercury libration and the liquid outer core

Review of the radar-and-spacecraft measurements of Mercury's forced libration in longitude, the implied decoupling of the mantle from the inner core, and the consequent constraint on a liquid outer core layer that supports the dynamo.
2026-04-28 · 6 min read · Reviewed by Corgan Studio, Inc.

Introduction

Mercury's interior structure is constrained primarily by three classes of measurement: gravity field (from spacecraft tracking), libration in longitude (from radar and imaging time series), and magnetic field (from in-situ measurements). The combination identifies a partially-molten core that contributes to the active dynamo and to the rotational dynamics of the planet. The libration measurement is particularly diagnostic: a fully-rigid Mercury would librate with one amplitude; a Mercury whose mantle is decoupled from its inner core through a liquid outer-core layer librates with a larger amplitude. This report reviews the libration measurement record and the implied interior-structure constraints.

The libration measurement

Mercury is locked in a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance: it rotates three times on its axis for every two orbits around the Sun. Solar gravitational torques on the equatorial bulge produce a forced libration in longitude with the orbital period. The amplitude of the libration depends on the moment of inertia of the librating body. If the entire planet librates as a rigid body, the amplitude is approximately 16 arc-seconds. If the mantle is decoupled from the inner core through a liquid outer core, only the mantle and crust librate, the effective moment of inertia is smaller, and the amplitude is approximately 35 arc-seconds.

Earth-based radar measurements by Margot et al. (2007, Science) measured the libration amplitude at 35.8 +/- 2.0 arc-seconds, conclusively favouring the liquid-outer-core model over the fully-rigid model. The result was the first definitive demonstration of a liquid layer within Mercury's interior and provided the dynamics-based justification for the active dynamo inferred from magnetic-field measurements.

MESSENGER refinement

MESSENGER's gravity-field measurement and altimetry-derived shape model refined the libration interpretation. The combined data set gives a moment of inertia C/MR^2 = 0.346, confirming the differentiated interior with a large iron-rich core. The mantle moment of inertia ratio Cm/C = 0.421 implies a mantle thickness of approximately 420 km - thinner than at the time of the original radar measurement and consistent with a core occupying approximately 80 percent of the planet's radius. The core is the largest core relative to the planet in the Solar System.

The solid inner core

Whether Mercury has a solid inner core, similar to Earth's, has been debated since the libration measurement. The libration amplitude alone is sensitive to the liquid outer core but not to the inner-core size or the inner-core decoupling. Genova et al. (2019) used MESSENGER gravity-field measurements and Mercury's obliquity to infer an inner core of approximately 50 percent of the core radius, with substantial uncertainty. A solid inner core is consistent with the dynamo model: heat-flow-driven convection in the liquid outer core can produce the observed dipole magnetic field.

Compositional implications

The high core-mass fraction implies that Mercury is enriched in iron relative to its nominal solar-composition formation expectation. Three classes of explanation have been proposed: (1) a giant impact early in Mercury's history that stripped a significant fraction of the original silicate mantle; (2) preferential loss of silicate material by the early energetic Sun's atmospheric heating; and (3) compositional inhomogeneity in the inner-Solar-System protoplanetary disc favouring iron-rich planetesimals near the inner edge. The giant-impact hypothesis is currently the most-cited but remains underdetermined; BepiColombo gravity-field and surface-composition measurements may discriminate between the alternatives.

Sulfur and the core melting curve

The liquid-outer-core model requires that the core remain partly molten at the present time. Pure iron freezes at temperatures consistent with a fully-solid Mercury core given reasonable interior heat-flow estimates. The presence of a liquid layer therefore requires either an exceptionally hot core or a depressed melting point through alloying with sulfur (Fe-S system) or other light elements. Estimated core sulfur content is approximately 5-10 weight percent, consistent with cosmochemical expectations.

BepiColombo extension

BepiColombo MPO will refine the libration amplitude by approximately a factor of two through high-precision spacecraft tracking and surface imaging. The refined value will tighten constraints on the mantle moment of inertia and, when combined with MPO gravity-field measurements, on the inner-core size. Additional constraints on internal structure come from the gravity-field harmonic content, the magnetic-field higher-order components, and the planetary-rotation pole position. The combined orbital data set is expected to converge the interior model to approximately 10 percent precision in the principal parameters.

Outlook

Mercury is the densest planet in the Solar System and the most-iron-rich. Its interior structure is therefore a high-leverage tracer for inner-Solar-System formation chemistry. The combination of pre-MESSENGER, MESSENGER, and BepiColombo data covers approximately three decades of progressive refinement. Further refinement beyond BepiColombo would likely require a future lander mission with seismometer, which has been proposed in concept studies but is not currently in development.
